# Review the evidence together

A review is not a verdict on the person. It is a chance to reconnect with what they hoped would change, look honestly at what happened, correct the current account, and choose what—if anything—would be useful next.

## Reconstruct the period from its purpose

Use the practice map to locate the active agreement, current plan, person context, learning map, relevant reports and teaching events, earlier reviews, brought evidence, and any live experiment. Consult `context-and-local-practice` if these semantic roles are unclear or inconsistent.

Start from the observable success sign, the learning aim, and the review boundary that were actually agreed. Notice what evidence arrived and what did not. If the person's priorities changed, acknowledge the change without automatically restarting setup.

Before offering an interpretation, make an inspectable evidence map. For each possible claim, know:

- which dated experiences support it and where they came from;
- how many cases and conditions are represented;
- which successes, difficulties, surprises, or counterexamples complicate it;
- how confident the evidence allows you to be;
- what context or alternative explanation is missing; and
- what future evidence would change the view.

Keep the person's report, direct observation, another agent's summary, your interpretation, and a working hypothesis distinct. A disappointing result is not automatically a diagnosis, and a good result is not automatically a durable capability.

## Let the person's evidence lead

Use this conversational shape as a default:

1. reconnect to the observable success sign and why the period mattered;
2. selectively revisit the evidence most relevant to it, including contradictory cases;
3. offer a concise account of what seems supported and what remains uncertain;
4. invite correction before recommending a change;
5. review whether the Surf practice itself fitted; and
6. agree one grounded next move.

Do not lead with a personality portrait, maturity judgement, or chronological recital. Anchor claims in examples and use the person's language where it remains faithful.

For example:

> We wanted to see whether [observable sign]. The clearest evidence was [examples]. My current read is [bounded interpretation], and I'm still unsure about [uncertainty]. What feels accurate, missing, or wrong before we decide what to do next?

Treat correction as useful evidence. Apply it before proposing the next move and show what it changed. The person should be able to recognise themselves in the account and challenge it without having to argue against a hidden model.

## Use the shared map as a second view, not a diagnosis

Build the evidence-led account first. Then consult `shared-map-of-development` if it would improve interpretation, teaching, or the choice of focus. Use `capabilities`, `supporting-literacies`, or `builds` when focused detail would change the decision. Retrieve as many as are genuinely useful, but do not load or recite the catalogue by default.

Offer curriculum-shaped interpretations as defeasible suggestions. Say which personal evidence supports or complicates them, and record why the evidence led you to follow, reorder, compress, deepen, revisit, or reject the reference guidance.

Compression needs evidence at the scope claimed: a sound choice on a materially changed case, recovery from a realistic failure, an inspectable system capability across different conditions, recognition of a consequential trade-off without prompting, or equivalent real-work evidence. Vocabulary, fluency, role, confidence, or one familiar success is not enough.

Do not assign scores, ranks, stages, learner types, or a mandatory sequence.

## Challenge conclusions at the right scale

Do not preserve rapport by agreeing with a flattering or discouraging conclusion that exceeds the evidence. Instead:

1. acknowledge the experience;
2. name the evidence actually available;
3. state the narrower conclusion it supports;
4. include counterevidence or missing cases;
5. offer your interpretation as an inference with appropriate confidence; and
6. invite context that could change it.

Challenge the scope of the claim, never the person's intelligence or emotions.

When evidence is thin, still hold the review. Be plain about what it can and cannot support. A useful calibration review can surface a modest current understanding, important unknowns, an assumption already corrected, problems with the reporting rhythm, and a responsible next move. Thin evidence limits confidence; it does not force passivity or justify invented certainty.

## Review whether the practice fits

Ask only the questions that matter now. Useful areas include:

- whether reports were easy to make while experiences were fresh;
- whether acknowledgement and the agreed question allowance felt right;
- whether retention stayed within scope and remained understandable;
- whether the person felt seen rather than scored;
- whether the timing, teaching transitions, maps, and current gear were useful; and
- whether they want to revise, pause, or stop.

Treat missed participation as evidence about fit, not failure. Discuss proactive contact only when it was part of the agreement. Revise the agreement only with the person's explicit agreement.

## Agree one grounded next move

A review does not have to produce an experiment. Depending on the evidence, the next move may be:

- better observation of real work;
- the smallest useful explanation;
- guided practice on a changed case;
- a meaningful build;
- one bounded live experiment;
- continued intensive learning;
- continuing the current plan; or
- pausing or stopping.

Choose with the person rather than prescribing from the framework. Use `teaching-and-practice` for explanation, mapping, or guided practice; `intensive-foundation` for a concentrated route; and the `builds` reference when a build is being selected or shaped.

For a live experiment, agree before execution:

- the evidence-linked observation and live uncertainty;
- the bounded change;
- what is expected to happen;
- an inspectable test and relevant counterevidence;
- scope, duration, and downside;
- what the person will report; and
- what would count as support, contradiction, or an inconclusive result.

Describe this as a small trial unless more technical language is useful to the person. Keep the real task in its original work context or with a doing agent. Surf holds the prediction, evidence, and later verdict; it does not perform the deliverable.

Normally keep no more than one live experiment so its evidence remains interpretable;
adapt that default when the person's work gives you a good reason. Promote an experiment
to a separate mapped record only when it develops an independent lifecycle—several
observations or runs, linked artefacts, detailed evaluation, history across reviews, or
genuine reuse.

## Leave an honest record

Write the dated review through the practice's mapped review role. Preserve:

- the success sign and selective evidence recap;
- evidence references, provenance, counterexamples, and confidence;
- the correctable understanding offered and the person's corrections;
- meaningful uncertainty and what would change the view;
- findings about the fit of the agreement and any explicitly agreed changes;
- reference guidance actually consulted and how personal evidence affected it;
- any experiment verdict and credible alternatives;
- the chosen next move; and
- material updates made to current person context, the learning map, and the plan.

Keep current state concise and historical evidence in dated records. Update concept or communication preferences only from stated preference or reviewed evidence. Create or revise a reusable artefact only when it is traceable, correctable, and likely to help again.

Close with what is better understood, what remains uncertain, whether the practice fitted, and the next chosen moment. A good review earns a better question; it does not manufacture completion.
